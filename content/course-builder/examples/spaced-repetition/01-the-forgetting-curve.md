# The Forgetting Curve

## Why this matters

If you do not know how fast you forget, you cannot plan when to review. Most people review too
late (after the memory is gone) or too early (while it is still effortless, which does little).

## Core ideas

### The forgetting curve (`forgetting-curve`)

Recall of new material drops steeply in the first day or two and then flattens out. The shape is
roughly exponential: most of the loss happens early. A memory you can recall today is not a
memory you will be able to recall next week unless something happens in between.

### What sets the decay rate (`decay-rate`)

The slope is not fixed. Material that is meaningful, connected to what you already know, and
has been successfully recalled before decays more slowly. Each successful recall after a delay
makes the next decay slower — this is the lever the rest of the course pulls.

## Worked example

You learn 20 new terms on Monday. Without review you might recall about half by Tuesday and a
handful by Friday. If instead you test yourself on Tuesday, most come back with effort, and the
curve after that Tuesday test is flatter than the one after Monday.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "I recognised it when I saw it" as "I remember it".
- Assuming the decay rate is a personal trait rather than something review changes.

## Recap

- Forgetting is fast at first, then slow.
- The slope depends on meaning, connections, and prior successful recall.
- Review is only useful if it changes the slope.
