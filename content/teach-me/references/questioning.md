# Asking questions

The learner wants to **use** ideas, not recite them. Every graded question tests whether an
idea can be applied; none tests whether a list was memorised.

## The seven question types

All short answers, all generated by the learner (never picked from options).

1. **Find the break** - a copy of something real with ONE thing quietly broken. What is broken,
   and what happens because of it?
2. **Fix it** - the smallest change that makes a broken thing work.
3. **Predict** - given this setup and this event, walk through what happens next and where it
   ends.
4. **Symptom to cause** - here is what someone saw going wrong; where do you look, and why?
5. **Pick and defend** - two designs, one situation: which one, and why? (An open answer with a
   reason, never a menu.)
6. **Tell them apart** - two real cases: which idea is each, and what gave it away?
7. **Push back** - a confident person says something wrong; answer them in a line or two.

**Draw the type at random, with a real random source** - `python scripts/progress.py draw`, or
any genuine random number from 1 to 7. Not by feel: a tutor choosing by feel drifts to the same
two comfortable types. If the drawn type genuinely cannot express the idea (e.g. "find the
break" for telling apart three meanings of a word), draw again.

The concept's `probe` in `assessment.md` is a *reference*, not a script. The question actually
asked is new each time; the `must_include` points describe the idea itself, so any type can be
graded against them. The grader always receives the question that was actually asked.

The draw always decides the question's *type*. The `probe` and `transfer` entries only supply
*material*: for a concept's first check the probe's situation is a good starting point, and for
a later review the `transfer` situation (when there is one) is the best test of use. Either way,
recast that situation in the drawn type - e.g. a transfer scenario drawn as "push back" becomes
a confident colleague defending the wrong choice in it.

## Where the material comes from

Pick whatever shows the idea most clearly:

- the course itself - the lesson's worked example, common mistakes, code inside the lesson;
- real files in the learner's own repository that embody the idea (shown as a copy in chat,
  never modified);
- a small realistic example written on the spot when neither fits.

## Discipline

- **Code comes with comments.** Any question containing code puts a short plain-language
  comment above each meaningful line saying what it *does* - never whether it is right. Finding
  the break stays the learner's job; reading the code should not be the obstacle.
- **Don't put the answer in the question.** Before asking, re-read the question against the
  `must_include` points. If the setup already states one of them (e.g. "it starts a fresh
  session for each stage" when the idea is that sessions carry no memory), rewrite it.
- **Don't spoil the check while teaching.** When explaining a concept, never use its `probe` or
  `transfer` situation as the example - the check must be a real act of generation.
- **No multiple choice for anything graded.** Picking from options tests recognition and hides
  exactly the mix-ups reviews exist to catch. Menus are fine for navigation ("next lesson or
  reviews first?") and for the optional confidence question only.
- **No verdict before the grade.** Between the learner's answer and the grade, do not call any
  part of it right, good, close or complete - not even a warm "yes, exactly" on the way. An
  early approving word ratifies an answer the grader may then fail, and the learner remembers
  the approval, not the grade.
- **Plain language.** No jargon without an explanation in the same sentence. If the learner
  asks for explanations in another language, explain in it - but keep questions, rubrics and
  grading in the course's language so every answer is graded the same way.
- **Optional confidence question.** Before grading, you may ask "how sure are you - guessing,
  fairly sure, or certain?" (a menu is fine here). Note it in the log next to the grade:
  confident misses are the misconceptions most worth revisiting.
